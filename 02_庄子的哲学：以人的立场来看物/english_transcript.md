### Restoring the True Face of Laozi's Philosophy

In the true philosophy of water dripping through stone, water never treats the rock as an enemy, a rival, or even an object of confrontation; piercing the stone is not a premeditated calculation, but a natural unfoldment. This natural unfoldment is the cosmic order of nature itself—this is the Dao.

Writing slowly and lecturing steadily across decades until something is penetrated has never been our objective, because we never view an obstacle before us as an adversary to be conquered by dripping water; if that were our intent, dripping water would be far too slow—why not simply become a strongman and haul the rock away? Although they were separated by a considerable span of time, they forged a unified philosophical lineage. Whenever we examine the foundational thinking of Daoist masters and the intellectual bedrock of Daoist spirituality, it is virtually impossible to separate it from Zhuangzi.

Zhuangzi looked at reality with the fresh, unjaded eyes of a child, raising boundless questions rather than preaching answers from an ivory dais; he posed questions and responded with childlike wholeness—a true artist who spoke through allegories, parables, and endless mythic narratives—a supreme literary artist.

I wish to emphasize two concepts in Zhuangzi's philosophy: the first may sound unfamiliar and somewhat tongue-twisting, where three out of five characters are the same word for material things—his philosophical dictum that "the creator of things is not a thing" (wù wù zhě fēi wù)—a truly pivotal concept in Zhuangzi's thought.

What does "the creator of things is not a thing" mean? The first "wu" functions as a verb, denoting the creative force that brings things into being; the "creator of things"—the one who generates material phenomena—is not itself a material object. "The creator of things is not a thing"—can you grasp the essence of what I mean? The transcendent power that causes things to become things is not itself a physical thing. In other words, we ought to become spiritual creators, and a creator is never a mere object—a profound concept that we must reflect upon deeply.

In the "Autumn Floods" chapter, he expounds a concept which, phrased in contemporary terms, urges us to view things from the human perspective, rather than evaluating human life from the perspective of material things.

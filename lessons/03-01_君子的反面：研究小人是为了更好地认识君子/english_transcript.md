Hello everyone, I am Yu Qiuyu, and let us continue our lecture. We are now about to embark on a very intriguing yet challenging subject: after having discussed the Way of the Junzi (the exemplary person), we must examine its opposite—the Xiaoren (the petty person). From this point forward, if this course were to have an overarching title, it would be "The Realm of Xiaoren."

When I discussed the personality structure of Chinese culture earlier, I pointed out that the personality structure of Confucianism is the Junzi. Within the Way of the Junzi, I outlined nine core essentials and four difficulties. As for the nine essentials, let me recap them briefly: Junzi cherishes virtue; the virtue of the Junzi is like wind; Junzi brings out the best in others; Junzi is harmonious without colluding; Junzi is serene and open-hearted; Junzi holds to the Golden Mean; Junzi observes propriety; Junzi is not a mere utensil; and Junzi knows shame. When discussing the four difficulties, I covered the friendships of the Junzi, the fame of the Junzi, the hypocrisy confronting the Junzi, and the tribulations of the Junzi.

The Counterpart Group to Junzi: Xiaoren

Once these nine essentials and four difficulties have been covered, in the conventional sense, my discourse on the Way of the Junzi would be complete. However, we undoubtedly must face its antithetical group known as the Xiaoren—the realm of the petty person. If we do not understand them, our study of the Way of the Junzi remains incomplete. In Confucius's discourse on the Junzi, virtually every principle is drawn in contrast with the Xiaoren; how could we possibly evade this very opposition established by our sage so long ago?

Throughout the process of discussing the Way of the Junzi, groups of Xiaoren invariably emerge dimly before our eyes. Each time they appear, we gain renewed admiration for Confucius and his companions for their deliberate design. To put it in modern colloquial terms: Want to know what a Junzi is like? Just look at a Xiaoren and you will know. By examining the positive from the negative, the Junzi uses a series of comparative formulations to draw an unmistakably clear boundary between the Junzi and the Xiaoren.

Confronted with such a demarcation, what tactic would the Xiaoren adopt? It is very simple: they disguise themselves as Junzi, and then shove the true Junzi into the camp of the Xiaoren. As a consequence, those celebrated aphorisms of Confucius are ironically often uttered by the mouths of Xiaoren—something that the great Junzi Confucius likely never anticipated at the time. Confucius may have had some inkling of concern, but he believed that "the masses cannot be deceived," or as we say today, "the public's eyes are sharp and discerning"; therefore, the collective crowd would always distinguish Junzi from Xiaoren. However, what he did not realize was that the masses are all too easily deceived, and the eyes of the crowd are, for the most part, far from sharp.

After Confucius, throughout Chinese history there has continuously flourished a large contingent of very peculiar individuals: they are Xiaoren appearing in the guise of Junzi. Their influence is immense, yet they are exceptionally hard to identify and virtually impossible to purge. This outcome also stems from an academic reason: although Xiaoren were repeatedly mentioned by way of comparison, they were almost never studied systematically. Why? Because this is an elusive and indeterminate group, making research extraordinarily difficult. Anyone engaged in scholarly research knows that the hardest subject to investigate is one that is elusive and shifting, which is precisely the nature of Xiaoren. Moreover, many Junzi are reluctant to plunge themselves for long into a quagmire that is both elusive and profoundly unsettling to the spirit.

Yet this issue must be addressed, and so I summoned up my courage and knitted my brows to make up for this historical void. Many years ago, I wrote an essay specifically investigating Xiaoren; when it first appeared, it was titled "Dark Corners of History," serving as the final piece of "Notes from Mountain Dwelling." Later, after being expanded, the essay was retitled "Xiaoren of the Earth"—earth and petty persons forming a paired antithesis. Such groups of Xiaoren appearing across the land exist everywhere, and this essay exerted a profound influence, being reprinted in numerous venues.

Interestingly, when I visited Taiwan, I met Master Cheng Yen of the Buddhist Compassion Relief Tzu Chi Foundation, that renowned Buddhist leader who frequently coordinates disaster relief and medical charity across the global Chinese community. She told me: "After your essay on Xiaoren was reprinted in Taiwanese newspapers, I clipped it out and pasted it into my notebook." Here was a Buddhist master whose life is dedicated to exploring benevolence and human virtue, yet she placed great weight on this negative phenomenon. Some commented that this was the very first essay to systematically investigate the negative personality archetypes in Chinese culture, which is indeed high praise.

Studying Xiaoren to Better Understand Junzi

However, I must make clear to everyone that my purpose in studying Xiaoren is to better recognize and understand the Junzi. The Xiaoren is merely a stepping stone for us to climb upon—to jump where? To ascend toward the Junzi. The Xiaoren ought to be negated, and through the process of examining the problem of Xiaoren, we should gradually cultivate ourselves into Junzi. Perhaps many historical scholars took an aloof stance, leaving Xiaoren without rigorous and systematic inquiry; therefore, I have decided to change course. I resolved to dive in and investigate thoroughly, exposing the series of issues surrounding them throughout this inquiry so that everyone can examine them closely. Although this study might sound somewhat peculiar—because you are entering a territory destined to draw historical and social controversy—as long as you study the Way of the Junzi, you cannot avoid this opposite side. Hence, we must study it conscientiously.

When speaking of Xiaoren, we first noted their indeterminate and elusive nature: who exactly are they? This group does not consist of heroes, nor are they necessarily heinous monsters or arch-villains. Their social status might be very humble, or it might be very prominent. In terms of educational background, they might be illiterate, or they could well be erudite scholars.

It is difficult to label them simply as good or bad people. Yet because of their existence, many vivid historical figures gradually became paralyzed, blurred, and irritable; many straightforward historical events turned chaotic, ambiguous, and sordid; many harmonious human relationships progressively became tense, awkward, and perilous; and many resonant historical propositions deteriorated into dimness, turmoil, and absurdity. They wield such colossal impact, yet if you ask whether they possess any clear political doctrine, they have none; none of their actions leave behind clear, accountable footprints; they never intend to bear responsibility toward anyone, and indeed it is impossible to hold them accountable. They are a cloud of foul and murky air that cannot be dispersed yet leaves no trace; they are an indeterminate flutter of whispers, glances, and grimaces.

When you finally become enraged and gather thunderous force to launch an assault against them, you find to your astonishment that these whispers and grimaces are actually standing right alongside you, expressing anger alongside you, and you suddenly discover that your target of attack has vanished. If you decide to ignore them and turn away, that murky current of foul air once again curls continuously and inescapably around you. Can you imagine the kind of people I am describing? They possess no conviction and no stance, yet they can ruin everything; when they are about to face attack, they are suddenly found standing on the attacker's side; yet they persistently surround you, leaving you with boundless fury and no one to direct it toward.

I believe that throughout history, when many iron-willed politicians and military commanders were finally on the verge of departing this world, what they hated most was not necessarily their overt, declared adversaries, but rather those faces that had once offered them countless splendid phrases and genial smiles—faces of whom one could never ultimately decipher whether they were friends or foes. In their dying moments, the trembling lips of those statesmen and generals struggled to utter a single word: Xiaoren. Yes, Xiaoren—that is the very protagonist we begin to examine in depth today. That concludes our lecture for today.

## Appendix: Summary of the Nine Core Tenets of the Junzi

Before delving into the "Realm of Xiaoren," Professor Yu Qiuyu systematically delineated the nine core essentials of the Confucian Junzi personality in preceding lectures. These nine tenets establish the definitive benchmarks of the ideal character in Chinese culture, serving as the mirror through which petty persons are identified and personal cultivation is realized:

1. **Junzi Cherishes Virtue (君子怀德)**: Virtue is what makes an authentic human being. The Analects notes: "The Junzi cherishes virtue; the petty person cherishes land and comfort." While the Xiaoren fixates on immediate material gains, clan real estate, and physical security, the Junzi anchors life in moral conscience and lofty spiritual values, grounding personal worth in ethical transcendence.
2. **Virtue of the Junzi is Like Wind (君子之德风)**: Solitary self-cultivation alone is insufficient. Confucius observed: "The virtue of the Junzi is like the wind, and that of the petty person is like grass; when the wind blows across the grass, it cannot but bend." True virtue must possess radiant social influence and momentum, inspiring the moral climate of the broader society with transformative grace.
3. **Junzi Brings Out the Best in Others (君子成人之美)**: Gazing upon others with eyes of sincere celebration. The Analects teaches: "The Junzi fosters the good in others, not what is base; the petty person does the opposite." Possessing a generous and benevolent soul, the Junzi rejoices in and aids the noble achievements of fellow human beings, rather than succumbing to envy, sabotage, or kicking others while they are down.
4. **Junzi is Harmonious Without Colluding (君子周而不比)**: Genuine public solidarity over factional conspiracy. In Confucius's words: "The Junzi is all-embracing and does not form cliques; the petty person colludes and is not all-embracing." The Junzi unifies broadly based on universal righteousness and common good, rejecting sectarian nepotism and conspiratorial cabals fueled by narrow self-interest.
5. **Junzi is Serene and Open-Hearted (君子坦荡荡)**: Untroubled and fearless like a clear sky. As recorded in the Analects: "The Junzi is serene and open-hearted; the petty person is forever anxious and restless." Because the Junzi acts with total transparency and an untarnished conscience, they are liberated from paranoid calculations, dwelling in calm fortitude; the Xiaoren, racked with deceit and greed, lives in perpetual dread.
6. **Junzi Holds to the Golden Mean (君子中庸)**: Mastering the dynamic golden balance amidst polarized extremes. The Doctrine of the Mean stresses "embodying the Mean at the fitting moment" (时中). Zhongyong is neither lukewarm compromise nor mediocre neutrality; it is the profound wisdom of perceiving intricate tensions and finding the exact, optimal equilibrium in human life.
7. **Junzi Cultivates Propriety (君子有礼)**: Reverence as a living form, uniting ritual with aesthetic grace. Confucius taught: "Without mastering propriety, one cannot establish oneself." Propriety (Li) is not superficial formalism, but the dignified, external expression of deep inner reverence for life, boundaries, and civilization.
8. **Junzi is Not a Mere Utensil (君子不器)**: Refusing utilitarian reduction and instrumental servitude. The Analects famously proclaims: "The Junzi is not a utensil." Tools serve singular, fixed mechanical functions. The Junzi steadfastly refuses to become a disposable cog or a slave to utilitarian greed, maintaining an expansive humanistic outlook and a sovereign, free soul.
9. **Junzi Possesses a Sense of Shame (君子知耻)**: Shame as the essential bottom threshold of human character. The Doctrine of the Mean states: "To know shame is close to courage." In a culture rooted in moral consciousness, true courage begins with the capacity to confront one's own transgressions. Knowing shame safeguards moral boundaries; when shame is abandoned, one descends directly into shameless depravity.
